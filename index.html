How we invest

Investment strategy

We invest in listed equities with a medium to long-term view. Every position is the result of research, open debate and a group decision, never of one person’s conviction alone.

Our objective is to grow capital over the medium to long term through a portfolio of listed stocks and exchange-traded funds, built on fundamental research rather than short-term market movements.

We look for good businesses at prices that make sense, and we are prepared to wait. Understanding a company well matters more to us than reacting to the mood of the market, and we revisit every position as the facts change.

Ideas follow a fixed procedure. A member researches a company and presents a pitch covering the thesis, the valuation and the risks. The group challenges it and the decision is put to a vote. A majority decides whether we buy, hold off or pass, and the reasoning is written down.

Our principles

Four rules we hold ourselves to, whatever the market is doing.

Research comes first

No position without a pitch built on fundamental analysis and financial data.

Patience over noise

A decision is judged by its thesis, not by a day’s price move.

Every idea is challenged

Questions and disagreement are part of the process. A pitch that cannot be challenged is not ready.

The majority decides

Each decision is put to a vote and the result stands, whoever brought the idea.

Key ratios explained

Twenty ratios we use most: what each one tells you and what it can hide. Related terms are in the Glossary below.

  • P/E ratio

    Price-to-earnings ratioShare price ÷ earnings per share

    ShowsHow much investors pay for each euro of profit.

    HidesMisleads for cyclical firms, where earnings peak or collapse.

  • P/B ratio

    Price-to-book ratioShare price ÷ book value per share

    ShowsPrice against the net assets on the balance sheet.

    HidesSays little for asset-light businesses such as software.

  • EV / Sales

    Enterprise value to salesEnterprise value ÷ revenue

    ShowsValue against revenue, useful when profit is still small.

    HidesIgnores how profitable the revenue is.

  • EV / EBITDA

    Enterprise value to EBITDAEnterprise value ÷ (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation)

    ShowsCompares companies with different debt levels and tax.

    HidesIgnores capital spending, so it flatters heavy industries.

  • P / FCF

    Price to free cash flowMarket value ÷ free cash flow

    ShowsThe price paid for the cash a business really generates.

    HidesOne delayed investment can make a year look better than it is.

  • PEG ratio

    Price/earnings to growth ratioP/E ÷ expected earnings growth

    ShowsPuts the P/E in context of how fast profit is growing.

    HidesDepends on growth forecasts, which are often wrong.

  • Gross margin

    (Revenue − cost of sales) ÷ revenue

    ShowsHow much of each euro of sales is left after the direct cost of the product.

    HidesLeaves out running costs, so a high gross margin can still lose money.

  • Operating margin

    Operating profit ÷ revenue

    ShowsPricing power and how well costs are controlled.

    HidesNot comparable across sectors; a grocer is not a software firm.

  • Net margin

    Net income ÷ revenue

    ShowsThe share of sales that ends up as profit for shareholders.

    HidesOne-off items, tax and interest can swing it from year to year.

  • FCF margin

    Free cash flow marginFree cash flow ÷ revenue

    ShowsHow much of sales turns into real cash.

    HidesLumpy: one big investment year can make it look weak.

  • ROA

    Return on assetsNet income ÷ total assets

    ShowsHow much profit a company makes from everything it owns.

    HidesLow by nature for asset-heavy businesses such as utilities.

  • ROE

    Return on equityNet income ÷ shareholders’ equity

    ShowsThe profit earned on the money shareholders have put in.

    HidesDebt inflates it; a heavily indebted firm can show a high ROE.

  • ROCE

    Return on capital employedOperating profit ÷ (total assets − current liabilities)

    ShowsProfit made on all the capital in the business, debt included.

    HidesGoodwill from past acquisitions can drag it down.

  • ROIC

    Return on invested capitalOperating profit after tax ÷ invested capital

    ShowsWhether growth creates value above what the capital costs.

    HidesEasy to distort with write-offs and acquisitions.

  • Debt / equity

    Total debt ÷ shareholders’ equity

    ShowsHow much of the business is funded by borrowing.

    HidesNormal levels differ a lot between sectors.

  • Current ratio

    Current assets ÷ current liabilities

    ShowsWhether a company can pay its bills over the next year.

    HidesA high figure can hide stock that will not sell.

  • Interest cover

    Operating profit ÷ interest paid

    ShowsHow comfortably profit covers the cost of debt.

    HidesLooks fine until profit falls, then drops quickly.

  • Net debt / EBITDA

    Net debt to EBITDA(Debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA

    ShowsHow many years of profit it would take to repay debt.

    HidesLooks safe at the top of a cycle and dangerous at the bottom.

  • Dividend yield

    Annual dividend per share ÷ share price

    ShowsThe cash income received for each euro invested.

    HidesA very high yield can mean the price has fallen because a cut is coming.

  • Payout ratio

    Dividends ÷ net income

    ShowsHow much of its profit a company pays out.

    HidesA high figure leaves little room to keep paying if profit falls.

Glossary

Plain definitions of the terms investors use.

Alpha
The return a portfolio earns above its benchmark after allowing for risk. A measure of skill rather than of market movement.
Asset allocation
How a portfolio is split between asset types such as shares, bonds and cash. It drives a large part of the portfolio's risk and return.
Balance sheet
A snapshot of what a company owns and owes at one date: its assets, its liabilities and the owners' equity.
Bear market
A long fall in market prices, commonly defined as a drop of 20% or more from a recent high.
Benchmark
An index we measure ourselves against, such as the S&P 500, to show whether our choices added anything.
Beta
How much a share tends to move compared with the overall market. A beta above 1 means it moves more than the market.
Book value
A company's assets minus its liabilities, as shown on its balance sheet. What would be left for shareholders on paper.
Bull market
A long period of rising market prices.
Capital gain
The profit from selling an investment for more than it cost.
Cash flow statement
Shows the cash that came into and left a company over a period, split into operations, investing and financing.
Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)
The average yearly growth rate of a figure such as revenue or profit over a period, as if it had grown at the same pace every year.
Compound interest
Earning returns on past returns as well as on the original amount, so growth builds on itself over time.
Correlation
How closely two investments move together. Low correlation between holdings helps spread risk.
Current ratio
Current assets divided by current liabilities. Shows whether a company can pay its bills over the next year.
Debt-to-equity ratio
Total debt divided by shareholders' equity. A higher figure means the company relies more on borrowed money.
Diluted earnings per share
Profit per share after counting every share that could be created from options and convertible securities. A stricter version of earnings per share.
Discounted cash flow
A valuation that adds up the cash a business is expected to produce, reduced to today's value because money later is worth less than money now.
Diversification
Spreading money across different investments so that one failure does not sink the whole portfolio.
Dividend
A share of a company's profit paid out to its shareholders, usually in cash.
Dividend payout ratio
The part of a company's earnings paid out as dividends. A very high ratio can be hard to sustain.
Dividend per share (DPS)
The total dividend a company pays for each share it has issued over a year.
Dividend yield
The yearly dividend divided by the share price. The cash return before any change in the price.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, whatever the price, so that purchases are spread over time.
Drawdown
The fall from a portfolio's highest value to its lowest before it recovers. A measure of how bad the bad periods were.
Earnings per share
A company's profit divided by its number of shares. The profit attributable to each share.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting charges.
Enterprise value
What it would cost to buy the whole business: market capitalisation plus debt, minus cash.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund. A fund that holds a basket of assets, often an index, and trades on an exchange like a share.
Expense ratio
The yearly cost of owning a fund, shown as a percentage of the money invested. Lower costs leave more of the return to the investor.
Free cash flow
The cash a company produces after paying to run and maintain the business. What is left for its owners.
Gross margin
The share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the product or service, before running costs.
Growth stock
A share in a company expected to grow sales and profits faster than average, often priced at a premium.
Hedge fund
A privately run investment fund that uses flexible strategies, such as short selling and borrowing, in pursuit of returns.
Hedging
Taking a position that offsets the risk of another investment, a bit like insurance against a loss.
Hurdle rate
The minimum return an investment must offer to be worth making.
Income statement
Shows a company's revenue, costs and profit over a period.
Index fund
A fund that aims to match an index such as the S&P 500 rather than beat it, usually at low cost.
Inflation
The general rise in prices over time, which reduces what each euro can buy.
Interest cover
Operating profit divided by interest paid. It shows how many times over a company could pay its interest from profit.
Junk bond
A bond from a borrower with a low credit rating. It pays a higher yield because the risk of default is higher.
KPI
Key performance indicator. A measure a company uses to track how the business is doing, such as sales growth or customer numbers.
Liquidity
How easily an investment can be sold for cash without moving its price much.
Margin of safety
Buying at a price well below our estimate of value, so mistakes do not become losses.
Market capitalisation
The total value of a company's shares: the share price multiplied by the number of shares.
Moat
A lasting advantage, such as a brand, a cost edge or high switching costs, that protects a company's profits from competitors.
Net asset value
The value of a fund's assets minus its liabilities, usually shown per unit or share.
Net debt
Total debt minus cash and cash equivalents. It shows what a company would still owe if it used all its cash to repay debt.
Net income
A company's profit after all costs, interest and taxes.
Net margin
Net income divided by revenue. The share of every euro of sales that ends up as profit for shareholders.
Next twelve months (NTM)
Figures that look ahead twelve months, based on analysts’ forecasts rather than reported results.
Operating margin
Operating profit as a share of revenue. Shows how much a company keeps from each euro of sales before interest and tax.
Price-to-book ratio
The share price divided by book value per share. Compares what the market pays with what the balance sheet shows.
Price-to-earnings ratio
The share price divided by earnings per share. How much investors pay for each euro of profit.
Quantitative easing
When a central bank buys bonds to push down interest rates and put more money into the economy.
Quick ratio
Liquid assets, leaving out inventory, divided by current liabilities. A stricter test than the current ratio.
Rebalancing
Adjusting a portfolio back to its target mix after price moves have shifted the weights.
Return on equity
Net profit divided by shareholders' equity. How much profit a company makes on its owners' money.
Return on invested capital
The profit a business earns on the money put into it. A high figure over many years usually points to a strong business.
Shares outstanding
The number of shares currently held by investors, including those held by insiders. Used to turn company totals into per-share figures.
Sharpe ratio
A portfolio's return above a risk-free rate, divided by its volatility. Shows how much return was earned for each unit of risk.
Short selling
Selling a borrowed share in the hope of buying it back cheaper. The loss can be larger than the amount first invested.
Stock split
A company divides its shares into more shares at a lower price. The company's total value does not change.
Total return
The full return on an investment: the price change plus any dividends or interest received.
Trailing twelve months (TTM)
Figures that add up the last four reported quarters, giving a full year that is always up to date.
Undervalued
Trading below our estimate of what the company is worth.
Unrealised gain
A profit on paper, on an investment that has not been sold yet.
Value stock
A share that looks cheap compared with the company's earnings, assets or cash flow.
Volatility
How much a price moves up and down. Often used as a measure of risk, though it is not the same as losing money.
Weighting
The share of a portfolio held in a single investment.
Working capital
Current assets minus current liabilities. The money a company has available to run day to day.
Yield
The income an investment pays, as a percentage of its price. It covers interest or dividends.
Yield curve
A line showing interest rates for bonds of different lengths. When short-term rates are higher than long-term ones, it is called inverted.
Zero-coupon bond
A bond that pays no interest along the way. It is sold at a discount and pays its full face value at the end.

How a pitch night works

Each pitch is a thorough presentation built on fundamental analysis and financial data, supported by technical analysis.

  1. Step 1

    The thesis

    The business, its financial data and why the market has it wrong.

  2. Step 2

    Valuation

    Multiples, forecasts and the price we would pay, with technical analysis for entry.

  3. Step 3

    Risks

    Market sentiment, headwinds and what would invalidate the thesis.

  4. Step 4

    Questions and vote

    The group challenges the pitch, then votes to buy, hold off or pass.
